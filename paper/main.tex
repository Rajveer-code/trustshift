\begin{abstract}
\noindent Deployment evaluation implicitly assumes that a larger distribution shift means greater
risk; we find this does not hold uniformly across four real deployment shifts. We introduce
\textbf{TrustShift}, a single protocol measuring discrimination, calibration, subgroup reliability,
and operating-point performance, applied to clinical risk prediction (NHANES$\rightarrow$BRFSS),
mental-health text classification (Kaggle$\rightarrow$Reddit/Twitter), mortgage-approval prediction
(temporal and cross-state shift over 42M U.S.\ loan records), and network-intrusion detection
(CIC-DDoS2019$\rightarrow$CICIDS2017). A lending model under measured covariate shift
(domain-classifier AUC up to $0.80$) improves in ranking (AUC) while its macro-averaged
operating-point performance stays essentially flat (within $0.02$) even as its positive-class F1
alone falls by up to $0.078$: a single metric does not tell the whole story, and shift magnitude
alone did not predict which one would move. A mental-health model at comparable domain-classifier
magnitudes loses up to $0.39$ AUC. Cheap, largely label-free probes -- prevalence change, a domain
classifier, an importance-reweighting check -- recover a mechanistic diagnosis for some shifts and
report \emph{inconclusive} for others when evidence is insufficient. A network-intrusion
detector holds a
near-ceiling in-domain AUC ($0.998$, capture-session-disjoint split) while collapsing to $0.68$ on
deployment traffic whose attack mechanisms are entirely absent from training. Post-hoc
recalibration cuts calibration error by one to two orders of magnitude in every domain and
generally leaves discrimination and subgroup reliability unrestored (security's AUC moves only
$+0.035$, short of recovering its $0.31$ collapse). We contribute a protocol, a four-domain
benchmark, and a release in which every reported number traces to a committed result file.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} distribution shift $\cdot$ deployment auditing $\cdot$ model
calibration $\cdot$ subgroup reliability $\cdot$ shift diagnosis $\cdot$ trustworthy machine
learning

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Consider three machine-learning systems at the moment of deployment. A clinical risk model moves
from a survey cohort to a national screening population; its discrimination barely changes, and it
looks fine---yet its probability estimates become substantially miscalibrated, so the risk numbers
it reports are wrong. An intrusion detector moves from one year's attack corpus to the next; its
in-domain AUC sits near the ceiling ($0.998$, measured under a capture-session-disjoint train/test
split), and it looks flawless---yet it collapses on deployment traffic whose attack mechanisms are
entirely absent from its training data. A mortgage-approval model moves across years and states
under a distribution shift large enough to be detected almost perfectly by a classifier trained to
spot it---its ranking of applicants (AUC) even improves, while its fixed-threshold approval
decisions shift as the underlying approval rate moves.

Three systems, three intuitions overturned. We argue these are not isolated accidents but three
faces of one deployment phenomenon. The one that looked fine failed; the one that looked perfect
failed; the one that looked most shifted did not. Why? Answering that question is the subject of
this paper, and the answer is not the magnitude of the shift alone.

Deployment evaluation is dominated by a single in-domain number---accuracy, or AUC---treated as
evidence of readiness. When shift is considered at all, it is treated as a scalar severity: bigger
shift, bigger risk, rather than as a mechanism with its own trustworthiness consequences. Clinical
prediction has a mature external-validation literature; fairness-under-shift has been examined in
medical imaging~\citep{schrouff2022diagnosing}; tabular shift has dedicated
benchmarks~\citep{gardner2023tableshift}; general shift benchmarks span vision and
language~\citep{koh2021wilds}. Each answers ``how much does accuracy drop under shift?'' None
answers the cross-domain question our three vignettes raise: \emph{are deployment failures of
trustworthiness domain-specific accidents, or do they follow rules that hold across
domains---and can those rules be read before deployment?}

We answer it with one protocol applied to four deliberately dissimilar domains (the three above
plus mental-health text classification). The protocol jointly measures discrimination, calibration,
subgroup reliability, and fixed-threshold operating-point performance, attempts a staged, largely
label-free mechanistic diagnosis of each shift, and quantifies what a remediation ladder restores.
Holding the protocol fixed across clinical tabular data, social-media text, mortgage records, and
network traffic separates what is general from what is idiosyncratic, and lets us ask directly:
\textbf{how reliably can deployment-shift magnitude and context explain machine-learning failure
fingerprints across trustworthiness dimensions, and how can mechanism-aware diagnostics guide
deployment auditing?} Across these four domains, scalar shift-magnitude measures alone do not
consistently explain which axis fails; deployment context, evaluated through a staged diagnostic
protocol that explicitly reports \emph{inconclusive} rather than forcing every shift into a
category, carries information magnitude misses. Whether shift \emph{mechanism} specifically
outpredicts shift \emph{magnitude} is a narrower question this paper tests directly and does not
resolve in the affirmative.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A joint deployment-trustworthiness audit.} A single protocol measures
  discrimination, calibration, subgroup reliability, and fixed-threshold operating-point
  performance together rather than one axis at a time, so that axes shown to move independently are
  not missed by a single-metric evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
  \item \textbf{Evidence that scalar shift magnitude alone is insufficient.} Within the evaluated
  settings, scalar measures of marginal distribution change (a domain-classifier AUC, a prevalence
  shift) do not consistently explain which trustworthiness axis fails or by how much
  (Section~\ref{sec:meta}); a lending model at large measured covariate shift stays robust in
  ranking while its fixed-threshold operating performance moves, and a mental-health model at a
  comparable domain-classifier magnitude collapses on every axis.
  \item \textbf{Deployment-context heterogeneity beyond magnitude.} Where prior audit history for a
  deployment population exists, that population's identity explains substantially more of the
  observed failure variation than scalar shift magnitude does---evidence that deployment context,
  not only shift magnitude, is worth measuring before deployment.
  \item \textbf{A staged, largely label-free diagnostic protocol.} Cheap probes---prevalence
  change, a domain classifier, and an importance-reweighting check---recover a mechanistic
  diagnosis for some shifts and explicitly report \emph{inconclusive} for others when the evidence
  does not support one, rather than forcing every shift into a category
  (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
  \item \textbf{A remediation ladder.} Across all four domains, post-hoc recalibration is the
  cheapest repair and reliably restores calibration (Section~\ref{sec:res-remediation}); it does
  not restore discrimination or subgroup reliability lost to shift, which require target
  supervision.
  \item \textbf{A mechanism-aware case study.} Network-intrusion detection shows severe,
  mechanism-specific degradation on attack families entirely absent from training, reported here as
  a supporting case study, not as general evidence that shift mechanism outpredicts shift
  magnitude.
  \item \textbf{A reproducible four-domain benchmark.} Harmonized shift-pairs, standardized
  predictions, and the protocol implementation are released so that every number in this paper, and
  any new domain placed on the same axes, regenerates from a committed result file.
\end{itemize}

\section{The TrustShift Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

The protocol consumes, for each domain, a source model's predictions on an in-distribution test
split (\emph{source}) and on one or more deployment splits (\emph{target}), together with a
subgroup label per example. It runs in three stages -- a multi-axis trustworthiness audit, a staged
shift diagnosis (label-free screening first, a labeled confirmatory probe only where target labels
and adequate support overlap exist), and remediation -- realized as six reported quantities.

\paragraph{Axis 1: Discrimination.} AUC (macro one-vs-rest for multiclass domains), with DeLong
95\% confidence intervals~\citep{delong1988comparing}. We report
$\Delta\text{AUC} = \text{AUC}_{\text{source}} - \text{AUC}_{\text{target}}$.

\paragraph{Axis 2: Operating-point performance.} Macro-F1 at a fixed $0.5$ threshold, reported
alongside AUC because the two can move by different amounts or in different directions
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). For binary domains we additionally report positive-class F1 where it
diverges materially from macro-F1: macro-F1 averages both classes' F1 equally, so it can mask a
real, class-specific change that positive-class F1 alone reveals
(Section~\ref{sec:res-fingerprint}).

\paragraph{Axis 3: Calibration.} Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 equal-width bins) and Brier
score per split. ECE quantifies the gap between predicted confidence and empirical
accuracy~\citep{guo2017calibration}; a model can discriminate perfectly and still be badly
calibrated, and vice versa.

\paragraph{Axis 4: Subgroup reliability.} Per subgroup $g$, AUC$_g$; the subgroup gap
$G(\text{split}) = \max_g \text{AUC}_g - \min_g \text{AUC}_g$; and the \emph{gap change}
$\Delta G = G(\text{target}) - G(\text{source})$---the headline reliability quantity---with a
stratified bootstrap 95\% CI ($N=2000$). Subgroups are demographic where available (clinical:
age, sex, race, BMI; lending: race), platform-derived proxy classes for text, and \emph{operational}
(attack family) for intrusion detection. We call this subgroup-conditional reliability and, for
intrusion detection, never interpret it as demographic fairness (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Axis 5: Shift diagnosis.} Each shift is decomposed with three probes, not all equally
cheap. \emph{Label shift}: change in class prevalence $\Delta\pi$ (labels only; label-free at the
target). \emph{Covariate shift}: a domain classifier trained to separate source from target
examples on shared features, scored by out-of-fold cross-validated AUC ($\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}}$;
$0.5$ = no shift, $\rightarrow 1$ = severe)~\citep{rabanser2019failing} (label-free). \emph{Concept
shift}: importance-weighting the source examples by out-of-fold domain-classifier odds (with a
sampling-prior correction applied before clipping) and recomputing source AUC; if the reweighted
source AUC stays near the source AUC while the target AUC is far lower, covariate shift alone
cannot explain the drop~\citep{lipton2018label}. This probe requires target \emph{labels} (to
compute the target AUC it compares against) and is therefore not label-free; we additionally
require the reweighting to have adequate source/target support overlap, measured by effective
sample size (ESS), before treating its result as evidence -- when ESS falls below 10\% of the
source sample, we report the probe as non-adjudicable rather than asserting a conclusion from it.
When no probe produces adjudicable evidence above threshold for a target, or when the evidence for
one mechanism is directly contradicted by another measurement (e.g.\ a large $\Delta\pi$ where the
positive-class populations are measurably disjoint between source and target, contradicting label
shift's requirement that $P(X\mid Y)$ be stable), the diagnosis is reported as
\textbf{inconclusive} rather than forced into a taxonomy branch.

\paragraph{Axis 6: Remediation ladder.} On a 10\% stratified target calibration split we apply
temperature, Platt, and isotonic recalibration~\citep{zadrozny2002isotonic}, then re-measure
discrimination, calibration, and subgroup reliability on the remaining 90\%. Operating-point
performance is not currently re-measured post-remediation. This isolates what a cheap post-hoc fix
restores from what requires target supervision.

Full metric definitions, seeds, and estimator choices are fixed in the released configuration;
all reported numbers regenerate from committed prediction files. Algorithm~\ref{alg:trustshift}
states the protocol as executed.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{The TrustShift audit}
\label{alg:trustshift}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require source predictions $(\hat p, y, g)_{\text{src}}$; target predictions $(\hat p, y, g)_{\text{tgt}}$; shared features $X_{\text{src}}, X_{\text{tgt}}$
\Statex \textit{\textbf{Stage 1 --- Evaluate the four trustworthiness axes}}
\State $\Delta\text{AUC} \gets \text{AUC}_{\text{src}} - \text{AUC}_{\text{tgt}}$; \; ECE, Brier per split \Comment{DeLong CIs; 10 bins}
\State $\Delta\text{F1} \gets \text{F1}^{\text{macro}}_{\text{src}} - \text{F1}^{\text{macro}}_{\text{tgt}}$ \Comment{operating point, threshold 0.5}
\State $\Delta G \gets (\max_g\!-\!\min_g)\text{AUC}_g^{\text{tgt}} - (\max_g\!-\!\min_g)\text{AUC}_g^{\text{src}}$ \Comment{subgroup gap; bootstrap CI}
\Statex \textit{\textbf{Stage 2 --- Diagnose the shift type}}
\State $\Delta\pi \gets$ prevalence change; \; $\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}} \gets$ out-of-fold CV-AUC of a source-vs-target classifier on $X$
\State $w(x) \gets$ prior-corrected, out-of-fold domain-classifier odds; $\text{ESS} \gets (\sum w)^2/\sum w^2$
\State $\text{evidence} \gets \emptyset$
\If{$\text{ESS}/n_{\text{src}} \geq 10\%$ \textbf{and} $\text{AUC}^{\text{rw}}_{\text{src}} \approx \text{AUC}_{\text{src}} \gg \text{AUC}_{\text{tgt}}$} $\text{evidence} \gets \text{evidence} \cup \{\textbf{concept}\}$ \EndIf
\If{$|\Delta\pi| > 0.15$} $\text{evidence} \gets \text{evidence} \cup \{\textbf{label}\}$ \EndIf
\If{$\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}} > 0.65$} $\text{evidence} \gets \text{evidence} \cup \{\textbf{covariate}\}$ \EndIf
\If{$\textbf{label} \in \text{evidence}$ \textbf{and} positive-class populations measurably disjoint} diagnosis $\gets$ \textbf{inconclusive}
\ElsIf{$\text{evidence} = \emptyset$} diagnosis $\gets$ \textbf{inconclusive}
\ElsIf{$|\text{evidence}| = 1$} diagnosis $\gets$ the one mechanism in evidence
\Else{} diagnosis $\gets$ \textbf{mixed} \EndIf
\Statex \textit{\textbf{Stage 3 --- Remediate and re-measure}}
\State fit recalibration on 10\% target labels; re-measure discrimination, calibration, and
subgroup reliability on the rest \Comment{operating point not re-measured}
\Ensure $(\Delta\text{AUC},\, \Delta\text{F1},\, \text{ECE},\, \Delta G,\, \text{diagnosis},\, \text{restored axes})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We organize results as one argument: in-domain metrics do not predict deployment behaviour
(\ref{sec:res-indomain}); each domain exhibits a distinct failure fingerprint, and the available
shift evidence supports different levels of mechanistic interpretation across targets, with three
of five inconclusive under the current probes (\ref{sec:res-fingerprint}); scalar shift magnitude
does not explain that variation, while deployment context does, out of sample, for populations with
prior audit history (\ref{sec:meta}); a pooled aggregate can even reverse the sign of what most
individual deployment cells show (\ref{sec:res-aggregation}); and recalibration reliably repairs
calibration but not discrimination or subgroup reliability (\ref{sec:res-remediation}).

\subsection{In-domain performance does not transfer, and does not transfer uniformly}
\label{sec:res-indomain}

Every source model looked deployable: source AUC ranged from $0.77$ (clinical) to $0.998$
(security, measured under a capture-session-disjoint train/test split) and source ECE was at most
$0.26$. Deployment outcomes did not follow (Figure~\ref{fig:bars}, Table~\ref{tab:master}).
Mental-health classifiers lost $0.28$--$0.39$ AUC on new platforms; the intrusion detector fell
from AUC $0.998$ to $0.684$; the clinical model's ranking discrimination moved only slightly
($\Delta\text{AUC}=0.031$), though its operating-point performance (macro-F1) fell by $0.102$; and
lending models improved in ranking (AUC) on later years while their macro-averaged operating-point
performance stayed essentially flat (change within $0.02$) even as positive-class F1 alone fell by
$0.056$--$0.078$ as approval prevalence shifted. A single in-domain AUC dashboard would have rated
all four ``ready'' and would have missed the operating-point divergence in clinical and, on the
positive-class-F1 reading, in lending.

\subsection{Shift evidence and failure fingerprints}
\label{sec:res-fingerprint}

The four domains exhibit distinct failure fingerprints (Table~\ref{tab:fingerprint}), while the
available shift evidence supports different levels of mechanistic interpretation across the five
evaluated targets: NLP carries \textbf{mixed} evidence, security and clinical are
\textbf{inconclusive} under the current probes, lending temporal diagnoses as \textbf{covariate},
and lending geographic is \textbf{inconclusive}. For each target below we report the observed
fingerprint first, the available diagnostic evidence second, and the resulting confidence level
third.

\emph{Every axis fails together $\rightarrow$ evidence is mixed (label $+$ covariate, and concept
where adjudicable).} The NLP shift's diagnosis is \textbf{mixed}, not a single mechanism: the domain
classifier
separates platforms almost perfectly ($\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}} = 0.93$--$0.99$) and class prevalence
shifts sharply ($|\Delta\pi|$ up to $0.52$), so both covariate and label evidence fire for both
targets. The concept-shift reweighting probe additionally fires for Reddit, where source/target
support overlap is adequate (effective sample size $16\%$ of $n_{\text{source}}$): reweighting
barely changes source AUC ($0.990\rightarrow 0.989$) while target AUC is $\approx 0.60$, so
covariate shift alone cannot explain that drop. For Twitter, support overlap is too thin
(effective sample size $4\%$) for the reweighting probe to adjudicate, and we report it as such
rather than asserting a mechanism from it. Whichever probes fire, the outcome is the same:
discrimination collapses ($\Delta\text{AUC}$ $0.28$--$0.39$ across the four architectures tested),
calibration breaks (target ECE up to $0.27$), and the subgroup gap widens ($\Delta G$ up to
$+0.15$). All three axes fail together, for the source-selected primary model (RoBERTa) and every
other model tested.

\emph{Discrimination collapses on attack mechanisms absent from training $\rightarrow$
inconclusive by the current probes.} The intrusion detector holds a near-ceiling in-domain AUC of
$0.998$, measured on a train/test split that is disjoint at the capture-file level (the dataset's
own native ``-training''/``-testing'' file partition) rather than a random row split. The native
file/session split showed no evidence of excess cross-split duplication relative to within-split
duplication under our duplicate check: the across-split near-duplicate rate ($2.0\%$) was no
higher than the rate already present \emph{within} the training files themselves ($5.9\%$) or the
testing files ($2.2\%$); we do not claim this proves the split leakage-free, only that this
specific check found no evidence of excess leakage. Under this evaluation, AUC still falls to $0.684$
externally, and calibration error rises from $0.069$ to $0.646$. This is \emph{not} label shift in
the classical sense: raw (uncapped) class prevalence does shift substantially
($\Delta\pi=0.633$, source $77\%$ attack, target $14\%$ attack -- a larger shift than the training-
pipeline's engineered class balance would suggest), but label shift's requirement that the
positive-class conditional distribution $P(X\mid Y{=}1)$ stay stable is directly contradicted: all
five attack families present at deployment are entirely absent from the source training data (see
the label-space audit released with this benchmark), so the diagnosis is reported as
\textbf{inconclusive} rather than forced into a label-shift label. Per-family recall shows a family
that shares a label string with a training-side family (\texttt{ddos}) detected at only $0.37$,
alongside three families with no training-side counterpart at all detected near zero
(\texttt{goldeneye} $0.00$, \texttt{hulk} $0.0003$, \texttt{slowloris} $0.21$,
\texttt{slowhttp} $0.07$). We describe this observed pattern as an \emph{unseen attack-family
deployment shift}: the binary task (benign vs.\ attack) is unchanged, but the attack mechanisms
present at deployment were never represented in training. Whether this is best formalized as a
distinct taxonomy category alongside covariate/label/concept shift, or as a mixed/compound shift,
is left to the controlled mechanism-isolation experiment in future work; we do not assign it a
firm category here.

\emph{Ranking is robust, the operating point is nearly flat $\rightarrow$ evidence is covariate.}
The lending temporal shift diagnoses as covariate ($\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}}$ $0.69$--$0.80$) with no
label or concept component. Ranking discrimination \emph{improves} ($\Delta\text{AUC}$ $-0.012$ to
$-0.028$) and calibration is stable; macro-averaged operating-point performance stays essentially
flat (change within $0.02$) even as positive-class F1 alone falls by $0.056$--$0.078$ as the
approval prevalence moves ($\pi=0.850\rightarrow 0.759$) and the denied class becomes less rare, and
the race gap shrinks. The geographic experiment shows a different pattern: ranking discrimination
also improves ($\Delta\text{AUC}=+0.059$) and the race gap narrows ($-0.029$), but macro-averaged
operating-point performance degrades more than in the temporal experiment (macro-F1 falls by
$0.034$), even though its own shift is diagnosed \textbf{inconclusive}: no covariate probe has been
built for the geographic target (see Table~\ref{tab:probe-availability}), so the shared
ranking-improvement pattern is a numerical similarity to the temporal result, not a confirmed shared
mechanism, pending the within-domain analysis in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\emph{Calibration fails despite near-flat discrimination $\rightarrow$ evidence is inconclusive.}
The clinical shift's diagnosis is \textbf{inconclusive}: the only available covariate probe for
this domain is a score-distribution
proxy (target-side features are unrecoverable, Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) that does not
produce an $\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}}$, so no probe reaches the evidence threshold. The observed
failure is nonetheless clear: the source-selected primary model (FedAvg) shows a small but
statistically detectable ranking-discrimination shift ($\Delta\text{AUC}=+0.031$, $\approx 7.2$
source-test standard errors) that is nonetheless $3$--$4\times$ smaller than its calibration and
operating-point shifts: operating-point performance falls sharply (macro-F1 $-0.102$) and
calibration deteriorates sharply (ECE $0.168\rightarrow 0.276$; $0.41$ for the non-primary boosted
model). The age subgroup gap narrows for the primary model
($\Delta G=-0.066$, 95\% CI $[-0.113,-0.016]$) but \emph{widens} for the non-primary boosted model
($\Delta G=+0.029$, 95\% CI includes zero) -- subgroup reliability here is a property of the
(model, shift) pair, not of the dataset alone. A model whose AUC certifies it as unchanged is
producing risk estimates that are substantially miscalibrated, at a fixed operating point that has
also moved.

\subsection{The regularity is categorical, not a magnitude law}\label{sec:meta}

Treating each (domain, model, target) as a shift-point ($n=16$), we asked whether the
subgroup-reliability change is predictable from cheaper quantities. \emph{Because the observations
cluster strongly by domain (effectively four clusters), we interpret every regression in this
section as an exploratory cross-domain summary, not a fitted predictor.} With that caveat stated
first: across shift-points, gap change is associated with calibration change (slope $0.42$, 95\% CI
$[0.17,0.60]$, $R^2=0.41$) and with aggregate accuracy loss (slope $0.34$, CI $[0.27,0.44]$,
$R^2=0.82$). The tempting third association---subgroup loss with covariate-shift magnitude---has a
high cross-domain fit (slope $0.54$, $R^2=0.77$) but is a statistical trap, which we report against
ourselves.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!6,colframe=gray!50,title=\textbf{Unexpected finding: shift
magnitude is not deployment damage}]
The cross-domain regression of subgroup-gap change on covariate-shift magnitude
($\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}}$) has slope $+0.54$ with $R^2=0.77$---a fit that, taken at face value,
would license ``measure the shift; the bigger it is, the more subgroup reliability you lose.''
That reading is wrong. Within the lending domain, where covariate-shift magnitude rises from
$0.69$ to $0.80$ across deployment years, the same slope is \emph{negative} ($-0.10$): more
measured shift, less damage. The positive cross-domain slope is a two-cluster, aggregation-induced
artifact separating high-shift text from moderate-shift tabular data---an aggregate line drawn
through two
groups that individually tell the opposite story. Lending exhibits larger measured covariate shift
than many settings described as ``severe,'' while remaining fully robust on every axis measured
this way. This mattered to our own analysis: an earlier version of this work reported the $0.54$
slope as a finding before the within-domain check exposed it, and we retain it here precisely
because the mistake is instructive. \textbf{Shift magnitude and deployment risk are not
interchangeable}; the \emph{categorical} diagnosis (Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy}) provides a more
structured description of the observed deployment evidence, but the present data do not establish
that mechanism categorization predicts failure more accurately than scalar shift magnitude -- that
comparative question remains unresolved.
\end{tcolorbox}

We therefore report the cross-domain regressions (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}) as exploratory
synthesis over four domain-clusters, not as a fitted predictor, and rest the contribution on the
magnitude-insufficiency finding, not on a claim that mechanism categorization outpredicts it.

\paragraph{Deployment context explains far more of this variation than magnitude does, out of
sample.} If scalar shift magnitude does not explain the within-lending variation above, what does?
Using the same $447$-cell lending-temporal panel, we tested whether a state's own identity --
under 10-fold cross-validation, never trained and evaluated on the same fold -- predicts its
held-out cells better than the scalar shift score does. It does, by a wide margin, on every axis:
state identity reaches $R^2_{\text{out}}=0.62$--$0.84$ (discrimination $0.77$, operating point
$0.81$, calibration $0.80$, subgroup gap $0.62$), while $\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}}$ under the identical
cross-validation scheme reaches at most $R^2_{\text{out}}=0.06$ and is negative on three of six
axes. This is context evidence, not mechanism evidence -- shift mechanism is held constant
(covariate) throughout this panel -- and it comes with a sharp operational caveat that must be
stated alongside it, not separately: the state \emph{identifier} is available before any
deployment, trivially, but its \emph{predictive value} is not. Holding out an entire state never
seen in training instead, state identity's $R^2_{\text{out}}$ collapses to approximately zero
($-0.03$ to $-0.02$), exactly as a fixed-effect encoding must for a level it has never observed.
Deployment-context heterogeneity is therefore a real, generalizing, and substantial signal a scalar
shift score misses -- but only for a population with prior audit history, not as a universal
pre-deployment diagnostic (\texttt{state\_identity\_generalization.json},
\texttt{context\_generalization\_final.json}).

\subsection{Aggregation-induced reversal: a worked methodological lesson}\label{sec:res-aggregation}

The lending-temporal panel also illustrates a pitfall this paper's own thesis warns against: an
aggregate number can disagree with what is happening in the cells that compose it, in a way that
would mislead an auditor who checks only the aggregate. Pooling all lending-temporal predictions
for a target year and computing one macro-F1 delta -- the whole-year aggregate reported in
Section~\ref{sec:res-fingerprint} -- shows operating-point performance essentially flat to
slightly improving ($-0.003$ to $-0.019$ across years). Computed the other way, per (state, year,
seed) cell and averaged with every cell counted equally, the picture flips: $66$--$80\%$ of the
$49$--$50$ cells in each target year individually \emph{degrade} (unweighted mean effect
$+0.024$ to $+0.046$, median effect $+0.025$ to $+0.056$), yet the pooled aggregate improves in all
three years. The reconciliation is arithmetic, not mysterious: weighting each cell by its row count
reproduces the pooled aggregate to within $0.005$--$0.008$, because a handful of large states (FIPS
$12$/Florida, $6$/California, $48$/Texas) dominate the row-weighted total while an unweighted
per-state mean gives a state with a thousand loans the same vote as a state with a million. We call
this an \textbf{aggregation-induced reversal} rather than Simpson's paradox: the sign reversal
itself is confirmed, but the classical two-group and lurking-variable conditions for that specific
term were not formally verified here (\texttt{lending\_aggregation\_effect\_final.json}). The
practical lesson mirrors this paper's central claim at a smaller scale: a single summary number --
here, one pooled aggregate delta -- does not tell the whole story, and an auditor who checks only
that figure for a lending model would conclude operating-point performance is stable while a
majority of the populations it serves are individually seeing it degrade.

\subsection{Recalibration repairs calibration only}\label{sec:res-remediation}

Isotonic recalibration on a 10\% labeled target split reduced ECE by one to two orders of
magnitude in every domain: clinical $0.276\rightarrow 0.002$, lending $0.240\rightarrow 0.002$
(temporal), security $0.646\rightarrow 0.004$, NLP $0.111/0.260\rightarrow 0.020/0.062$
(Table~\ref{tab:remediation}; source-only primary models throughout, Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
In clinical, lending, and NLP, AUC and the subgroup gap moved by less than $0.012$---calibration is
cheaply repairable, discrimination and reliability are not. Security is the exception: isotonic
recalibration moves AUC by $+0.035$ ($0.684\rightarrow0.718$). We do not yet have a rows-level tie
count for the current (leakage-controlled, XGBoost) pipeline as we did for the earlier lightgbm
result, so we report this as an open question rather than repeat the earlier ``tie artifact''
explanation unverified for this model; either way, $+0.035$ does not recover the $0.31$ collapse.
Restoring discrimination requires target supervision---e.g., in the NLP domain, target fine-tuning
recovers far more than any post-hoc fix~\citep{pall2026cpfe}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What failed, and why.} The four domains produced four different failure patterns. NLP's
shift carries mixed evidence (both label and covariate probes fire, and the concept probe
additionally fires for one target), and every axis failed together: discrimination, calibration,
and subgroup reliability all collapsed. Security's shift is diagnosed \textbf{inconclusive} by the
current probes, yet discrimination and calibration collapsed together on the attack families absent
from training, and even the one family shared with training degraded substantially
(Section~\ref{sec:res-fingerprint}). Lending's temporal shift, diagnosed \textbf{covariate}, degraded
on none of the four measured axes; its geographic shift, also evaluated here but diagnosed
\textbf{inconclusive}, degraded operating-point performance while ranking still improved. Clinical's
shift, likewise inconclusive, affected calibration and the operating point far more than
discrimination. Which axes failed together, and how severely, differed by target; whether that
pattern is explained by shift \emph{mechanism} specifically, rather than by deployment context or
another factor the evaluated probes do not isolate, is the open question Section~\ref{sec:meta}'s
own direct test does not resolve in the affirmative.

\paragraph{The most practically important observation.} Maintaining high discrimination after
deployment did not imply trustworthy behaviour. The clinical model's ranking discrimination moved
only slightly while its probability estimates became substantially miscalibrated, and the lending
model stayed robust or improved on every measured axis despite measurable covariate shift. These
contrasting outcomes argue that deployment decisions should not rest on aggregate discrimination
alone, but should incorporate the multi-axis audit this protocol runs---which is cheap and, for the
covariate and label probes specifically, requires no target labels.

\paragraph{What practitioners should do differently.} The results support a concrete pre-deployment
routine, not a formally validated predictive decision rule: (1) screen with cheap, largely
label-free probes -- prevalence change, a domain classifier, and, where target labels exist, the
reweighting check; (2) characterize what evidence is actually available, since not every target
supports every probe (Table~\ref{tab:probe-availability}); (3) audit all four trustworthiness axes
rather than aggregate discrimination alone; (4) report \emph{inconclusive} honestly when the
evidence does not support a firm diagnosis, rather than forcing one; (5) budget a small labeled
target sample for recalibration, which was cheap and consistently improved calibration; and
(6) escalate to target supervision when discrimination or subgroup reliability remain degraded
after remediation, since recalibration alone did not reliably restore them. Monitoring aggregate
AUC alone, the most common practice, was the least informative signal in all four domains.

\paragraph{Outlook.} If this pattern continues to hold across additional domains, deployment
evaluation may need to shift its emphasis from measuring \emph{how large} a distribution shift is
toward auditing multiple trustworthiness axes directly, since scalar magnitude alone did not
consistently explain which axis failed in the settings studied here. Some shift evidence can be
screened before deployment using unlabeled target data, while other mechanisms require target
labels or remain inconclusive under the available probes (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). We do not
claim a universal law from four domains, nor that shift mechanism has been shown to outpredict
shift magnitude; we claim a reproducible pattern of axis-specific failure, a magnitude-insufficiency
finding, and a cheap protocol for testing both on a new domain. Establishing where mechanism-aware
diagnosis does and does not add information beyond magnitude is the natural next step, and the
benchmark is built to accept new domains.

\paragraph{What this evidence establishes, and what remains uncertain.} Framed against the four
hypotheses this paper set out to test: \textbf{H1} (different shift mechanisms produce
distinguishable failure fingerprints) is \emph{mixed} -- only one of four possible mechanism-pair
comparisons was both valid and informative enough to adjudicate. \textbf{H2a} (scalar shift
magnitude alone is insufficient to characterize the observed failure variation) is
\emph{supported}, with both in-sample (447 cells, every axis) and out-of-sample
(Section~\ref{sec:meta}) evidence. \textbf{H2b} (shift mechanism carries information beyond
magnitude about which axis fails) is \emph{not established} -- the direct test (mechanism-only vs.\
magnitude-only vs.\ both, leave-one-out cross-validated) returns negative out-of-sample $R^2$ for
every candidate model on the $n=10$ mechanism-comparable points available, which we read as
underpowered rather than as evidence against the hypothesis. \textbf{H3} (the staged audit is more
actionable than a scalar score alone) is \emph{not formally tested}; no comparison in this paper
measures actionability directly. These four statuses, not a comparative mechanism-beats-magnitude
claim, are this paper's actual scientific position.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Dataset-shift taxonomy.} The covariate/prior/concept-shift decomposition TrustShift's
diagnosis protocol is built on is not new: it follows the canonical taxonomy
of~\citet{morenotorres2012unifying}, the foundational treatment
in~\citet{quinonerocandela2009dataset}, and Shimodaira's importance-weighting correction for
covariate shift~\citep{shimodaira2000improving}. TrustShift does not propose a new taxonomy; it
operationalizes the existing one with label-free-where-possible probes and is explicit, via its
\emph{inconclusive} category, about where those probes cannot adjudicate.

\paragraph{Shift diagnosis and decomposition.} Our probes build on black-box shift
detection~\citep{rabanser2019failing} and label-shift estimation~\citep{lipton2018label}. The
domain-classifier probe (AUC$_{\text{dc}}$) is grounded in the $\mathcal{H}$-divergence bound
of~\citet{bendavid2010theory}, which relates target error to source error plus a source/target
divergence a classifier can estimate directly. The closest prior work is
DISDE~\citep{cai2023disde}, which decomposes an aggregate performance \emph{change} into
harder-seen-inputs, $P(Y\mid X)$-change, and new-input-region components with a formal estimator.
DISDE answers \emph{why aggregate performance moved}; TrustShift asks a different question --
\emph{which trustworthiness axis failed}, jointly across discrimination, calibration,
operating-point performance, and subgroup reliability -- and does not claim its categorical
diagnosis explains failure better than a decomposition method such as DISDE would; that comparison
is untested here.

\paragraph{Open-set, novel-class, and subpopulation shift.} Genuine open-set recognition -- new
classes entering the label space at test time -- is formalized by~\citet{scheirer2013toward}.
Security's deployment shift is deliberately \emph{not} described in those terms: the binary task
(benign vs.\ attack) is unchanged at test time, so no label-space expansion occurs. The closer
prior-work anchor is subpopulation shift~\citep{yang2023change}, which studies degradation on
subpopulations absent or rare in training while the label space stays fixed -- the same structure
as security's unseen attack families within the single positive class, and a framing we also draw
on for the deployment-context result discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\paragraph{Shift robustness benchmarks} such as WILDS~\citep{koh2021wilds} and
TableShift~\citep{gardner2023tableshift} quantify how much accuracy drops under shift, within a
modality; \citet{wiles2022fine} cross shift \emph{type} with method across
$\approx\!85{,}000$ models and are the reason we do not claim prior work studies shift one domain or
type at a time -- that claim would be false and directly checkable against their own results.
TrustShift differs from all three not in scale but in what it measures: calibration and subgroup
reliability alongside discrimination, jointly, on a small number of real, non-synthetic,
cross-modality deployment shifts, coupled to a staged diagnostic and remediation workflow rather
than a robustness leaderboard.

\paragraph{Calibration under shift.} \citet{ovadia2019trust} establish that predictive-uncertainty
calibration degrades under distribution shift and that post-hoc calibration methods can underperform
in that regime -- the antecedent our own calibration finding (calibration and the operating point
degrade far more than discrimination in three of four domains) builds on rather than rediscovers.
Our post-hoc recalibration step uses temperature, Platt, and isotonic
scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration,zadrozny2002isotonic}; we report Expected Calibration Error with
equal-width binning and note, following~\citet{roelofs2022mitigating}, that equal-mass binning has
lower estimator bias -- a sensitivity check this paper's released configuration is built to run but
has not yet executed. Calibration failure is not a cosmetic concern in a domain such as clinical
risk prediction, where miscalibrated probabilities directly mislead
decisions~\citep{vancalster2019achilles}.

\paragraph{Subgroup and fairness reliability under shift} has mostly been examined within single
domains; \citet{schrouff2022diagnosing} show fairness properties fail to transfer in medical
settings and that clinically plausible shifts violate the assumptions of single-mechanism
mitigations. We corroborate the multi-mechanism point across four domains: the subgroup gap moves
in different directions for different (model, shift) pairs within the same domain, not just across
domains. A recent survey~\citep{lin2024fairnessshiftsurvey} catalogues methods; we contribute an
empirical, cross-domain fingerprint rather than a new fairness-under-shift method.

\paragraph{Where TrustShift sits.} TrustShift is not a new dataset-shift taxonomy -- it uses the
existing one. It is not a formal performance-decomposition estimator -- it asks a different
question than DISDE. It is not a claim that shift mechanism outpredicts shift magnitude -- that
direct comparison, run in Section~\ref{sec:meta}, does not resolve in the affirmative. Its
contribution is the joint discrimination/calibration/operating-point/subgroup-reliability audit,
coupled to a staged, largely label-free diagnostic workflow that reports \emph{inconclusive} as a
first-class outcome, evaluated on four real, non-synthetic, cross-modality deployment shifts -- a
combination no single work cited above provides.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Four domains and sixteen shift-points are evidence, not proof; the cross-domain regressions are
exploratory synthesis over effectively four clusters, and we do not present them as a fitted
predictor. Subgroup semantics differ across domains by necessity---demographic for clinical and
lending, platform-derived proxy classes for text, and \emph{operational} attack families for
security---and we never interpret security's subgroup reliability as demographic fairness.
Clinical target-side subgroup analysis is limited to age: the released prediction arrays were
generated from a previously harmonized BRFSS dataset whose preprocessing pipeline is no longer
recoverable, so sex, race, and BMI analyses are not reproducible on the target side; age-based
analyses remain fully reproducible and constitute the primary clinical subgroup evaluation. The
clinical (source-only primary model, FedAvg) and lending subgroup gaps \emph{narrow} under shift,
which we attribute tentatively to changes in subgroup composition rather than to genuine fairness
improvement, and flag as a hypothesis; this narrowing is model-dependent, not domain-dependent --
the non-primary clinical boosted model's gap \emph{widens} under the identical shift ($\Delta
G=+0.029$), so subgroup reliability here is a property of the (model, shift) pair. The security
detector's near-ceiling in-domain AUC ($0.998$) is measured under a capture-session-disjoint
train/test split; a duplicate-row check found no evidence of excess cross-split duplication
relative to within-split duplication (we do not claim this proves the split is leakage-free). A
random row-level split of the same data (ignoring capture-file identity) gives a higher AUC of
$1.000$, reported alongside as a sensitivity comparison rather than as the primary number. We
treat the native-split $0.998$ as a genuine in-domain ceiling for whole-flow volumetric/reflection
features rather than a leakage artifact, though we
have not yet formally characterized \emph{why} the separability is so high. This study
examines four deployment settings rather than an exhaustive sample of machine-learning domains;
important modalities---especially vision foundation models and large language models, whose shift
behaviour may differ---remain to be tested, and the benchmark is designed to accept them.
Finally, all experiments run on a single workstation, bounding model scale.

\paragraph{Scope of the magnitude, context, and mechanism claims.} The context-heterogeneity result
(Section~\ref{sec:meta}) uses a bare state identifier as the only context variable; no economic or
regulatory state-level covariate was available in the current feature set, so we test whether
population identity carries signal, not what about a population carries it. The
magnitude-insufficiency claim (H2a) is evaluated against two proxies,
$\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}}$ and $|\Delta\pi|$; other severity proxies (e.g.\ population-stability
index, raw score drift) are not tested. The direct mechanism-vs-magnitude comparison (H2b) has only
$n=10$ mechanism-comparable points after excluding cells without both a valid mechanism label and
sufficient severity spread -- underpowered, not merely inconclusive, and every candidate model in
that comparison scores negative out-of-sample $R^2$; we do not treat this as evidence against H2b,
only as evidence the current data cannot adjudicate it. H3 (staged diagnosis more actionable than a
scalar score) has no formal test in this paper. The aggregation-induced reversal
(Section~\ref{sec:res-aggregation}) is demonstrated in lending only; whether the same weighting
effect recurs in the other three domains is untested. Clinical has no covariate-shift probe at all
-- the only available proxy never reaches $\text{AUC}_{\text{dc}}$ -- so its \texttt{inconclusive}
diagnosis reflects missing evidence, not evidence against covariate shift. NLP's remediation-stage
subgroup gap is undefined (\texttt{NaN}) for both targets in the released artifacts, a
data-generation issue not yet root-caused; the remediation claim in
Section~\ref{sec:res-remediation} is scoped to clinical and lending's subgroup-gap stability for
this reason.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Deployment shift does not degrade machine-learning trustworthiness uniformly: discrimination,
calibration, operating-point performance, and subgroup reliability can move independently, and even
in different directions, within the same deployment. Scalar marginal shift scores -- a
domain-classifier AUC, a prevalence shift -- were not sufficient, on their own, to explain which
axis moved or by how much in the settings studied here. Deployment context can carry additional
information beyond scalar magnitude when historical audit data for that population exist, though
this signal does not extrapolate to a population never audited before. Mechanism-aware diagnosis is
a promising organizing framework -- it recovers a specific diagnosis for some shifts and honestly
reports \emph{inconclusive} for others -- but its predictive superiority over scalar shift magnitude
has not been established by the direct test this paper runs. In-domain metrics predicted none of
the deployment outcomes we observed. We release the TrustShift protocol and benchmark so that new
domains, and the open question of when mechanism adds information beyond magnitude, can be tested
directly.
